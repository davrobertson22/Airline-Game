Six regional jets that earn their keep

Thirty types are listed above, four of them business jets, and most of them are wrong for you in any given week. Six repay the money: one for an empty balance sheet, one for a mature network, one that flatters itself badly on the stat table, and three that quietly do the work. Match seats to demand, range to mission, and only then argue about price.

Fokker 100 — the first jet you can actually afford

Nine million dollars buys one outright, which is precisely the wrong move when your bank shows ten. Lease instead at $22,000 a week and you have handed yourself 409 weeks — close to eight years of turns — before the payments add up to the sticker price. In the meantime you are flying 122 seats, the third-largest cabin in the regional fleet behind only the E195 and the E195-E2, both of which cost several times as much. Fuel is the surprise: 4.22 cents per seat-kilometre undercuts the Sukhoi Superjet 100 at $56,000 a week and the SJ-100 at $75,000.

What leaves your account each week is lease plus maintenance — and the Fokker arrives ten years old, so maintenance starts at $41,700 rather than the $27,800 base. That makes $63,700, which spread over 122 seats is $522 a seat. Only the Avro RJ85 holds a seat for less, and it burns more fuel. The catch is where it can go. Range stops at 3,170 kilometres, so nothing long. It wants 5,900 feet of runway, so nothing small. And 122 seats is a great many to fill on a thin spoke, where you pay $522 for the empty ones just the same. Give it dense secondary-city pairs, not hub feed.

Embraer ERJ-170 — the hub-feed workhorse

If one type on this list is mispriced in your favour, it is this one. Eighty seats, 3,735 kilometres, a 4,300-foot field requirement, $35,000 a week. Now look directly at the E175 next to it in the fuel order: 88 seats, the identical 3,735-kilometre range, and Embraer wants $60,000. That is $25,000 a week for eight extra seats — $3,125 per seat per week — while burning slightly more fuel, 3.82 cents against 3.75, and demanding 400 feet more runway. There is no reading of that trade where the E175 wins.

The ERJ-170 arrives six years old, so its maintenance starts at $25,960 — a touch above the factory-fresh E175's $25,600, nowhere near enough to rescue the newer jet's lease. Lease and maintenance together come to $60,960, or $762 a seat, and eighty seats is close to the natural size of a feed market — big enough to be worth the slot, small enough to fill on a Tuesday morning. The 4,300-foot runway opens secondary fields the E195 cannot touch at 5,900 feet. Range is the honest weakness: 3,735 kilometres rules out the long thin cross-border work, so pair it with something with legs. Buy at $18M once cash flow is genuinely steady, which the lease equals in 514 weeks.

Embraer E190 — long legs without the E2 price

Sooner or later a pair you want sits 4,500 kilometres apart and most of this list simply cannot reach it. The E190 does 4,537 kilometres, and only three regional airliners go further: the E195-E2 at 5,600, the E190-E2 at 5,460 and the Superjet 100 at 4,578. It leases at $55,000 and arrives five years old, so maintenance starts at $37,125: $92,125 a week, $808 a seat, for 114 seats at 3.73 cents per seat-kilometre, sixth on the table.

Set it beside the Superjet and the gap is embarrassing. The Sukhoi leases for $1,000 more, carries six fewer people and burns 4.66 cents; its lighter maintenance makes it $2,861 a week cheaper to hold, but the E190 burns about 78 cents less per kilometre and wins that back within about 3,700 km of flying a week. The real rival is the E190-E2: the same 114 seats, 5,460 kilometres, 3.08 cents and a 4,400-foot runway against the E190's 5,700, for $22,275 a week more. The E2 saves about 74 cents a kilometre, so it only pays once the aircraft flies roughly 30,000 km a week. Below that, the older jet is the better lease. One caution: fuel efficiency you can shrug at when the market sits at $1.45 a litre becomes the whole argument when the market drifts towards double that — the higher fuel climbs, the fewer kilometres the E2 needs.

BAe 146-200 — the short-field key

Below 4,000 feet of runway your airliner choices collapse to two, and only one of them carries a useful number of people. The 146-200 puts 112 seats into 3,900-foot fields that lock out almost everything else on this page: the Avro RJ85 needs 4,000 feet, the ERJ-170 4,300, the E195 a full 5,900. The only airliner that lands shorter is the Yakovlev Yak-40 at 3,300 feet, which seats 32 and burns 12.66 cents per seat-kilometre — nearly five times the E195-E2's 2.63, and the worst of any regional airliner. Only the two largest business jets, the Challenger 350 and G650ER, do worse.

The money is modest: $17,000 a week and $7M outright, though it arrives twelve years old, so maintenance starts at $45,408 and the fixed cost is $62,408, or $557 a seat — the third-lowest on this table, behind the RJ85 and the Fokker 100. Be disciplined about it, though. Where the runway allows an RJ85 instead, take the RJ85: 100 feet more concrete buys 4.47 cents rather than 4.73, and because the Avro arrives two years younger, its fixed bill is $5,008 a week lower too ($57,400). There is no trade-off at all. Treat the 146 as a key rather than a fleet — it unlocks airports, and its 3,650-kilometre range means those spokes can be surprisingly long ones.

Embraer E195-E2 — the efficiency leader you grow into

Nothing else in the regional fleet comes near 2.63 cents per seat-kilometre. The E190-E2 is next at 3.08 and burns 17% more; the worst regional airliner burns nearly five times as much. Put that on a 5,600-kilometre sector — the longest reach available to any regional airliner — with all 146 seats sold, and against a mid-table 4.09-cent aircraft you are roughly $11,900 better off per flight before you have sold a single ticket. That advantage grows every time the fuel market climbs above $1.45 a litre, which it does often, and by a lot.

Its lease and price are the highest of any regional airliner: $89,000 a week and $45M to buy, plus $39,600 in maintenance. Held together that is $128,600 a week, $881 a seat — steep, but still less per seat than the E175-E2's $1,051 or the SpaceJet M90's $1,157, which is exactly the argument for it. Purchase takes 506 weeks of lease payments to justify, so lease until the network stops changing shape. The genuine hurdle is the cabin. A 146-seat aeroplane is not a regional aircraft on a thin spoke, and each empty seat still costs $881; it wants markets where a narrow-body is nearly right, or feed dense enough to fill twice daily. Pleasingly, 4,850 feet of runway is less than a 90-seat CRJ-900 needs, so the size costs you no airports. See route economics before you commit.

Embraer E175-E2 — the efficiency trap

Read the fuel column alone and this looks like the third-best aircraft on the table: 3.21 cents per seat-kilometre, beaten only by the two larger E2s. Read the lease column and it inverts. Seventy thousand dollars a week across 90 seats is $778 per seat, while the E195 asks $60,000 across 124 seats, which is $484 — you are paying 61% more per seat for the privilege, and the fuel edge you bought is 0.32 cents per seat-kilometre. On a 1,500-kilometre sector with every seat sold, that edge returns about $432 a flight.

To be fair to it, the sums are not one-sided. The E2 arrives new while the E195 arrives five years old, so maintenance is far kinder, $24,600 against $41,850, and the total weekly hold is $94,600 versus $101,850. The trouble is what the money buys: 90 seats at $1,051 each, against 124 at $821. Thirty-four extra seats for $7,250 a week, about $213 each, is not a close decision. Nor can it lean on distance to redeem the burn, since it stops at 3,735 kilometres where its bigger siblings reach 5,460 and 5,600, and efficiency pays hardest over range. One case justifies it: a 4,700-foot field the E195 cannot use, serving a market that truly tops out near 90 passengers. Outside that window, the stat table is flattering you.
