Six turboprops worth a slot in your fleet

Thirty-two types is too many to evaluate honestly, so here are six that cover the decisions you will actually face: what to lease in week one, what to grow into, what to reach for when a runway is impossibly short, and what to walk past no matter how good its fuel figure looks. Match seats to demand first, then range to mission, then price.

Fokker F27 Friendship — the week-one workhorse

Third on the fuel table at 2.29 cents per seat-kilometre, and the two types ahead of it both lease for more than $43,000 a week. The Fokker asks $11,000. That is $212 for each of its 52 seats, and even after the $8,000 maintenance bill you are running $19,000 a week — $365 a seat, all in — for an aircraft that burns fuel within a whisker of a modern ATR. Against a $10M opening balance you can put three of these in the air, open six or seven routes, and still hold enough cash to absorb a bad fuel week without grounding anything.

The compromise is on the ground. It wants 5,300 feet of runway, the second-longest requirement of any turboprop here, beaten only by the Electra's 5,500. That rules out precisely the sort of small field where a cheap 52-seater would otherwise print money, so build your first network around proper regional airports rather than airstrips. Range is 1,900km, far beyond anything you should be flying with it, which at least means a misjudged market is still reachable. Buying outright costs $4M — 364 weeks of lease payments. Lease it now; revisit the maths when your balance is measured in tens of millions.

Short 360 — thin-market scalpel

Thirty-nine seats for an $8,000 lease works out at $205 apiece, and only four types in the game beat that number: the Convair 580 and DC-3 at $125, the An-24 at $173, the Electra at $192. Every single one of them burns at least 2.85 cents per seat-kilometre. The Short 360 burns 2.38, fourth-best of the 32. Cheap seats attached to genuinely cheap fuel is a combination that exists nowhere else on the list, and the 4,300ft runway requirement is ordinary rather than restrictive.

Run the all-in numbers against the Fokker and something useful falls out. Lease plus maintenance on the Short is $14,200 a week across 39 seats, or $364 each; on the F27 it is $19,000 across 52 seats, or $365. They cost effectively the same to operate per seat. So the choice between them is purely about demand — take the 360 wherever the market will not fill 52 seats, because in that case the extra thirteen are dead weight rather than upside. Its 1,178km range is the shortest of anything recommended here, which is no handicap at all; see route economics for why turboprop margins evaporate with distance.

Saab 2000 — the mid-game bridge

Compare it with the Xian MA600 and the case makes itself. Both burn 2.46 cents per seat-kilometre. The Xian carries 60 seats for a $31,000 lease; the Saab carries 58 for $15,000. You would be paying $16,000 a week — $832,000 across a year — for two extra seats and 1,268 fewer kilometres of range. At $259 a seat, only the Lockheed Electra offers cheaper seats at this size, and the Electra burns 3.69 cents to do it.

The Dash 8-300 is the other obvious rival, and it loses on every column: 56 seats to the Saab's 58, 2.85 cents to 2.46, a $17,000 lease to $15,000, $11,200 of maintenance to $9,600, and 1,558km of range to 2,868. Both cost $7M to buy. There is no configuration of routes in which you should prefer it. What the Saab's range actually buys is optionality — when a hub throws up an 1,800km market that would otherwise force you into a jet, this is the turboprop that can still reach it without adding a second aircraft class to your maintenance bill.

Dash 8 Q400 — the modern workhorse worth growing into

Everything on the Q400 points in one direction: volume. Ninety seats is more than any other turboprop bar the 104-seat Electra, and it burns 2.21 cents per seat-kilometre, the best figure in the entire category. The $43,000 lease looks brutal beside a $15,000 Saab until you divide it out at $478 a seat, against $564 on the ATR 72-600. Fold in maintenance — $16,800, the heaviest weekly bill of any turboprop — and the Q400 costs $664 per seat per week to the ATR's $744, while carrying twelve more passengers.

That is the whole argument, and it holds only if you fill the aeroplane. Ninety empty seats at $59,800 a week will bleed you faster than any fuel spike, so grow into this type from a hub with proven traffic rather than speculating with it — hub strategy covers how to manufacture that demand first. The 4,300ft runway requirement shuts you out of the small fields where the little types earn their keep, and 2,040km of range is generous for missions you should be flying under 800km anyway. Purchase is $22M, or 512 weeks of lease: a decision for a mature balance sheet, not week thirty.

CASA CN-235-300 — the trap

The fuel column lies about this one. At 2.42 cents per seat-kilometre the CASA sits fifth of 32, ahead of the ATR 42-600 and both Xians, and if that is the only figure you read you will lease it. Then the bill arrives: $52,000 a week for 51 seats, which is $1,020 per seat. The Fokker F27 carries 52 — one more — for $11,000. That $41,000 weekly gap is $2.13M over a year, handed across for nothing but a longer range you will never have a use for.

4,355km is the longest reach of any turboprop in the game and it is worthless on the sub-800km sectors where these aircraft beat jets in the first place. The purchase price compounds the joke at $26M — four million more than a 90-seat Q400. Its one honest virtue, an $8,000 maintenance bill, comes nowhere near covering a lease three times the size of the Saab 2000's. The same disease appears elsewhere: the Dornier 228 and Let L-410 both ask $895 a seat for nineteen passengers, and the Pilatus PC-12 wants $1,111 for nine. Efficiency you never fill is a statistic, not a profit.

DHC-6 Twin Otter 400 — the key to locked airports

Twelve hundred feet. That is the entire reason to own one, and it is reason enough. Only the Britten-Norman Islander matches that runway figure, and the Islander offers nine seats and 774km of range to work with; the Twin Otter gives you nineteen seats and 1,705km. Below 2,300ft — where the Dash 7 and the L-410 bottom out — the fleet list simply stops offering you anything, so an airport in that band belongs entirely to whoever bothered to lease the aircraft that can land there.

Nobody takes one for the economics. 3.82 cents per seat-kilometre ranks 26th of 32, the $14,000 lease is $737 a seat, and with $4,000 of maintenance you are spending $18,000 a week to move nineteen passengers — $947 each. Those numbers only work when there is no alternative, because an uncontested field lets you hold fares no AI carrier is in a position to undercut. Never fly it where something cheaper fits: a Cessna 208B needs 2,400ft, leases for $6,000 and burns 3.47 cents. Check the runway figure before you commit; if the strip takes the Cessna, the Twin Otter is just an expensive habit.
