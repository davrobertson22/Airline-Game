Six narrow-bodies worth building a network on

Fifty-two types is too many to think about. Six will build any network you care to fly. What follows moves from the jet you lease in week one to the one you grow into, and it includes a type new players reach for far too early. Every figure comes from the table above or from the game's own market screen, with the arithmetic spelled out so you can argue with it.

Airbus A319ceo — the first jet worth leasing

At $43,500 a week to lease, plus maintenance that starts at $53,100 because the frame arrives six years old, the A319ceo costs $96,600 a week — the lowest fixed cost of any narrow-body with 150 seats or more, and the cheapest sensible way to put 156 seats in the air. Its 6,900km range is actually longer than the A319neo's. That combination — small cabin, transcontinental legs, a modest 5,300ft runway — lets you open thin long routes from week one instead of waiting until you can afford something bigger.

Set it against the bargain-bin metal and the case closes. A 727-200 leases for $24,000, which looks like free money until you meet its maintenance: it arrives nearly eighteen years old and bills about $155,750 a week, so you are paying about $179,750 against the A319ceo's $96,600. That is $83,000 a week more, and for it you get 4.56 cents per seat-kilometre instead of 3.13 cents — a 46% fuel penalty — plus 2,080 fewer kilometres of range and 1,200 more feet of required runway. The 727's 189 seats are its only argument, and they are seats you must genuinely fill. Old airframes also age into rising bills and mechanical failures, so that maintenance figure is a floor rather than a ceiling. Older still, the jets whose lines closed in 1976 or earlier — the Caravelle, 727-100, DC-9-10 and their peers — cannot be leased at all. Lease two A319ceos before you lease anything cheaper.

Boeing 737-800 — the capacity step you can own

Buy this one rather than lease it. $28M puts 189 seats on your balance sheet permanently, and from then on the weekly bill is maintenance and nothing else — about $68,000, because it arrives six years old. Leased, it costs $68,000 plus that maintenance — about $136,000 all in, which is $39,400 more than an A319ceo for 33 extra seats and virtually identical fuel, 3.07 cents against 3.13 cents.

That is the whole decision: you are buying capacity, not economy. Take it only when your A319s are turning passengers away, because 33 seats you cannot fill burn exactly as much fuel as full ones. Where the -800 earns its keep is a maturing hub with several dense pairs — 5,765km covers nearly any domestic mission, and 5,700ft of runway asks nothing unusual of your airports. It is also the cheapest way into ownership at a useful size, since the MAX 8 sitting above it costs $49M for the same cabin, three-quarters more capital. Own two -800s outright and your fixed costs are about $136,000 a week for 378 seats, which is the sort of structure that survives fuel drifting towards $2 a litre.

Boeing 737 MAX 8 — the utilisation play

If you fly your aircraft hard, this is where a modern fleet starts. It carries exactly the 737-800's 189 seats at 2.67 cents per seat-kilometre against 3.07 — 13% less fuel for the same cabin — and 6,570km of range from an ordinary 5,700ft runway puts them almost anywhere you want. (The MAX 9 and MAX 10 burn less per seat still, at 2.44 cents, if your markets are bigger.) The catch is the bill: a $92,000 lease plus $62,400 maintenance comes to $154,400 a week — it arrives factory-new, so that is the real figure — and $18,400 more than a leased 737-800.

Run the numbers before you sign. On a 4,000km sector the MAX 8 burns about $20,150 of fuel; the -800 burns about $23,200 carrying the same 189 people. You save roughly $3,060 per flight, so you need about six sectors of that length a week to close the $18,400 gap. Utilisation is the entire argument. Park a MAX 8 on two round trips a week and it is a worse aeroplane than the -800 it replaced; work it across a busy hub and nothing at that size earns more. Judge it on the schedule you actually intend to fly, not the one you imagine — fleet planning is a scheduling problem before it is a shopping problem.

Airbus A321neo — the efficiency leader to grow into

Sooner or later your best pair fills a MAX 8 and you need more seats without more departures. The A321neo answers that: 244 seats at 2.36 cents per seat-kilometre, beaten only by its own XLR sibling, with 7,400km of range from 5,950ft of runway, barely more than a 737-800 needs. Nothing about the aircraft limits where you can send it. It simply insists on a market deep enough to fill it.

Against the obvious Boeing alternative it is not a close call. A MAX 10 leases at $115,000 with $70,000 maintenance, $185,000 a week; the A321neo totals $195,400. For $10,400 more you gain 14 seats, 1,660 additional kilometres of range and 3.3% better fuel. Treat it as the jet you grow into on three or four trunk routes rather than a fleet standard, though — 244 seats on a market that supports 180 is the classic way to turn the most efficient narrow-body available into a loss-maker, and load factor beats fuel burn every single time. Work through route economics before you commit one to a pair you have not already saturated with smaller aircraft.

Airbus A321XLR — the expensive mistake

This is the aircraft new players lease first, and it is nearly always the wrong week to do it. It tops the efficiency table at 2.35 cents per seat-kilometre — one hundredth of a cent better than the A321neo. For that hundredth you pay $144,000 in weekly lease and $75,000 in maintenance, $219,000 all in, against the neo's $195,400. The $23,600 gap buys precisely one thing: 1,300 extra kilometres of range, at roughly $1.2M a year.

It also demands 7,500ft of runway, the longest requirement of any modern type on the table, which quietly removes some of the airports you wanted it for. Do not expect the old long-range narrow-body to undercut it, either. A 757-200 reaches 7,222km with 239 seats off 5,500ft, but it arrives twelve years old: $74,500 of lease plus $137,600 of maintenance is $212,100 a week, only $6,900 less than the XLR, and it burns about $1.65 more per kilometre, so a single 7,000km sector gives that saving back. Lease the XLR when you have identified a specific ultra-long pair no other narrow-body reaches and the demand to fill 244 seats on it. Lease it merely because it sits at the top of an efficiency ranking and you have bought the most expensive rounding error in the game.

Boeing 757-300 — the density specialist

Two hundred and ninety-five seats is the largest cabin here, and the reason to want it has nothing to do with fuel. At 2.68 cents per seat-kilometre the 757-300 sits well behind the neos. What it wins on is fixed cost per seat: an $87,000 lease plus maintenance that starts at about $109,000 (it arrives six years old) totals $196,000 a week for 295 seats, whilst an A321neo costs $195,400 for 244. You move 51 more passengers for about $600 a week more, and it costs $36M to buy against the A321neo's $58M.

The bill arrives as discipline. That maintenance charge is the heaviest of the six jets on this page, it wants 6,900ft of runway, and 6,295km keeps it regional rather than intercontinental. Above all, the traffic has to be genuinely there: carrying those 51 extra passengers 3,000km costs about $6,500 more in fuel than an A321neo flying the same sector, roughly $127 a head, so your fare must clear that margin. Run it at 60% and you are moving 177 people whilst paying the fuel bill for 295 — worse than a smaller jet flown full. On a saturated trunk pair out of a mature hub, nothing else on this table comes close.
