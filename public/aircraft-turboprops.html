Turboprop Aircraft Guide

All turboprops in Tailwinds compared: seats, range, lease cost, fuel burn, and per-seat economics. The cheapest way to fly short, thin routes.

Turboprops are the most misunderstood aircraft in Tailwinds. New players skip them because they look slow and small; experienced players quietly build entire profitable sub-networks on them. Below roughly 800 kilometres, nothing else in the game touches their trip costs — the fuel table below makes the point better than any argument.

Their weekly leases are the lowest in the game, which also makes them the lowest-risk way to experiment. A failed turboprop route costs you pocket change; a failed widebody route can cost you the company.

How to choose

The ATR 72 and Q400 are the modern workhorses — the ATR cheaper per trip, the Q400 bigger, longer-legged and cheaper per seat. Both cruise at the same turboprop speed. Many of the older types in this table are 1940s–60s propliners whose lines closed in 1976 or earlier: no lessor stocks those, so they are buy-only, and they arrive decades old. Smaller 19–40 seat types unlock genuinely tiny markets (island hops, mountain fields, remote communities) that no jet can serve economically. Range is rarely the constraint; matching seats to a thin market is the whole art.

Six turboprops worth a slot in your fleet

Fifty-two types is too many to evaluate honestly, so here are six that cover the decisions you will actually face: what to lease in week one, what to grow into, what to reach for when a runway is impossibly short, and what to walk past no matter how good its fuel figure looks. Match seats to demand first, then range to mission, then price.

Saab 2000 — the week-one workhorse

Fifty-eight seats, 2,868 kilometres of range and a $15,000 lease. The Saab arrives ten years old, so its maintenance starts at $14,400 rather than the $9,600 base, and the whole aircraft costs you $29,400 a week — $507 a seat. Among turboprops of 50 seats or more that a lessor will rent you, only the Hawker Siddeley 748 holds a seat for less. A bill that small lets you find out which of your first routes are real without betting the company on any of them.

The 748 is the one to weigh it against. It is $1,800 a week cheaper to hold, but it arrives nearly seventeen years old, burns 4.86 cents per seat-kilometre to the Saab's 4.57, and at that age its maintenance climbs steeply each year and mechanical failures come far more often. The fuel gap alone closes the difference at about 10,800 km a week. The Saab's 4,200ft runway requirement is ordinary, and its range is far beyond anything you should be flying with it — which means an 1,800km market that would otherwise force you into a jet is still within reach. Buying outright costs $7M, 467 weeks of lease payments. Lease it now; revisit the maths when your balance is measured in tens of millions.

Short 360 — thin-market scalpel

Thirty-nine seats for an $18,664 weekly bill — $8,000 of lease and, because it arrives twelve years old, $10,664 of maintenance. That is $479 a seat, and among the turboprops you can lease only the Hawker Siddeley 748 holds a seat for less. It also burns 4.43 cents per seat-kilometre, less than anything else with 40 seats or fewer, where the 748 burns 4.86. Cheap seats attached to cheap fuel at this size exist nowhere else on the list, and the 4,300ft runway requirement is ordinary rather than restrictive.

Run the all-in numbers against the Saab 2000 and something useful falls out: $479 a seat against $507. They cost much the same to operate per seat. So the choice between them is purely about demand — take the 360 wherever the market will not fill 58 seats, because in that case the extra nineteen are dead weight rather than upside. Its 1,178km range is the shortest of anything recommended here, which is no handicap at all; see route economics for why turboprop margins evaporate with distance.

Dash 8-300 — the short-field all-rounder

At 4.22 cents per seat-kilometre the Dash 8-300 burns less per seat than any other turboprop leasing for under $20,000 a week, and it gets 56 seats into a 3,500ft strip. It leases at $17,000 and arrives ten years old, so maintenance starts at $16,800: $33,800 a week, $604 a seat. Against the Saab 2000 that is $4,400 a week more, for two fewer seats and 1,558km of range rather than 2,868. What you buy is 700 feet of runway and better fuel: the Dash burns about 29 cents less per kilometre, which covers the gap at roughly 15,200 km a week.

The runway is the point. The Fokker F27 is $3,161 a week cheaper and burns almost exactly the same per seat, but it needs 5,300ft — the Dash lands in two-thirds of that. The ATR 42-600 gets into 3,600ft on better fuel, but at $48,800 a week it needs some 40,000 km of flying to repay its extra $15,000, far more than short turboprop sectors ever add up to. If your network grows into the small regional fields where turboprops earn their keep, this is the aircraft that follows you there. Both it and the Saab cost $7M to buy.

Dash 8 Q400 — the modern workhorse worth growing into

Everything on the Q400 points in one direction: volume. Ninety seats is more than any turboprop a lessor will rent you bar the 110-seat Ilyushin Il-18, and it burns 3.07 cents per seat-kilometre, the best figure in the entire category. The $43,000 lease looks brutal beside a $15,000 Saab until you divide it out at $478 a seat, against $564 on the ATR 72-600. Fold in maintenance — $19,824, since it arrives six years old — and the Q400 costs $698 per seat per week to the ATR's $744, while carrying twelve more passengers.

That is the whole argument, and it holds only if you fill the aeroplane. Ninety empty seats at $62,824 a week will bleed you faster than any fuel spike, so grow into this type from a hub with proven traffic rather than speculating with it — hub strategy covers how to manufacture that demand first. The 4,300ft runway requirement shuts you out of the small fields where the little types earn their keep, and 2,040km of range is generous for missions you should be flying under 800km anyway. Purchase is $22M, or 512 weeks of lease: a decision for a mature balance sheet, not week thirty.

CASA CN-235-300 — the trap

The fuel column lies about this one. At 3.76 cents per seat-kilometre the CASA sits fourth of 52, ahead of the ATR 42-600 and both Xians, and if that is the only figure you read you will lease it. Then the bill arrives: $52,000 a week for 51 seats, which is $1,020 per seat. The Fokker F27 carries 52 — one more — for $11,000. Even after the old Fokker's age-inflated maintenance, the gap is $30,800 a week, $1.6M over a year, handed across for nothing but a longer range you will never have a use for.

4,355km is the longest reach of any turboprop that entered service after 1960, and it is worthless on the sub-800km sectors where these aircraft beat jets in the first place. The purchase price compounds the joke at $26M — four million more than a 90-seat Q400. Its one honest virtue, a $9,440 maintenance bill, comes nowhere near covering a lease more than three times the size of the Saab 2000's. The same disease appears elsewhere: the Dornier 228 and Let L-410 both ask $895 a seat in lease alone for nineteen passengers, and the Pilatus PC-12 wants $1,111 for nine. And the third-best fuel figure in the category belongs to the Vickers Vanguard, at 3.63 cents, which no lessor will touch: its line closed in 1964, so it is buy-only and arrives thirty years old with a $96,250 weekly maintenance bill. Efficiency you never fill is a statistic, not a profit.

DHC-6 Twin Otter 400 — the key to locked airports

Twelve hundred feet. That is the entire reason to own one, and it is reason enough. Only the Britten-Norman Islander matches that runway figure, and the Islander offers nine seats and 774km of range to work with; the Twin Otter gives you nineteen seats and 1,705km. Below 2,300ft — where the Dash 7 and the L-410 bottom out — the fleet list simply stops offering you anything, so an airport in that band belongs entirely to whoever bothered to lease the aircraft that can land there.

Nobody takes one for the economics. 7.12 cents per seat-kilometre ranks 46th of 52, the $14,000 lease is $737 a seat, and with $4,000 of maintenance you are spending $18,000 a week to move nineteen passengers — $947 each. Those numbers only work when there is no alternative, because an uncontested field lets you hold fares no AI carrier is in a position to undercut. Never fly it where something cheaper fits: a Cessna 208B needs 2,400ft, leases for $6,000 and burns 5.38 cents, though it seats only fourteen. Check the runway figure before you commit; if the strip takes the Cessna and the market is small, the Twin Otter is just an expensive habit.
