Narrow-Body vs Wide-Body Aircraft

A narrow-body airliner has one aisle and a wide-body has two. That difference in the width of the cabin decides how many people an aircraft can carry, how far it usually flies, what it costs, and which routes an airline can use it on.

By the Tailwinds team · Last updated November 9, 2026

The definition

A narrow-body airliner has a single aisle, a cabin less than four metres wide and up to six seats in each row. The Boeing 737 and the Airbus A320 are the familiar examples. A wide-body has two aisles and seven or more seats in each row, in a fuselage typically five to six metres across. Economy cabins seat seven to ten abreast. The widest airliner of all, the 7.14-metre Airbus A380, is one of the few that can take eleven.

How the wide-body came about

Every jet airliner had one aisle until 1970, when the Boeing 747 entered service as the first wide-body. The first twin-engined wide-body, the Airbus A300, followed in 1974.

For years the largest long-haul aircraft had three or four engines, partly because of a safety rule. From 1953, US regulations kept twin-engined aircraft within 60 minutes' flying time of an airport where they could land on one engine, which ruled out most ocean crossings. The rules, known as ETOPS, were relaxed as engines proved more reliable: to 120 minutes in 1985, when TWA flew a Boeing 767 from Boston to Paris under the new limit, and to 180 minutes in 1988. The Airbus A350 is certified for 370 minutes. A twin burns less fuel than a three- or four-engined aircraft of the same size, so most wide-bodies built today have two engines.

What the second aisle buys

Seats. A wider cabin holds more people in the same length of aircraft, and two aisles let them board and leave in reasonable time.

Range. A larger airframe carries more fuel. In the chart below, drawn from the aircraft in Tailwinds, no narrow-body passes 9,000 km and the longest-range wide-bodies fly twice that.

Freight. A wide-body's hold carries far more cargo under the passengers' feet, and the same cross-section converts readily into a freighter, with room for two eight-foot pallets side by side.

Two things stand out. A wide-body costs roughly three to five times as much to lease as a narrow-body. And it does not move a seat more cheaply: in a typical cabin, which on a wide-body includes roomy business-class seats, fuel per seat-kilometre is a little higher than on the newest narrow-bodies. What the wide-body offers is more seats on one flight and a range the narrow-body cannot match.

The line is blurring

The newest narrow-bodies fly routes that used to need two aisles. The Airbus A321LR has a range of 4,000 nautical miles (7,400 km), which made it the first of the A320 family able to fly any transatlantic route. The A321XLR reaches 4,700 nautical miles (8,700 km). Iberia put it into service in November 2024 and flew it from Madrid to Boston on the 14th, and United has ordered it to replace its Boeing 757-200s.

For an airline, this opens long routes that have too few passengers to fill a wide-body. It is one reason single-aisle jets fill the order books. On 7 October 2025 the A320 family passed the 737 as the most-delivered jetliner in history, at 12,268 aircraft to 12,262.

How airlines choose, and how to choose in Tailwinds

The rule in the game is the one an airline's planners use. Fly the smallest aircraft that can cover the distance and that the route will fill. Add flights before adding size, because passengers value a choice of departures. Move to a wide-body when the route is beyond a narrow-body's range, or when several narrow-body flights a day still leave passengers behind.
