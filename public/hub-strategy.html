Hub Strategy: Building Networks That Feed Themselves

Where you put your hub decides which routes can work, which aircraft suit them and how much competition you face. This guide covers choosing a first hub, growing it, and deciding when a second one is worth the money.

By the Tailwinds team · Last updated November 9, 2026

Why hubs matter more than routes

A route on its own earns whatever its two cities generate between them. A route into a hub also carries passengers connecting to every other route you fly from that hub. The arithmetic is what makes hub-and-spoke networks work. Ten spokes into one hub are ten routes to fly, but they connect 55 city pairs, because each spoke city can reach the hub and every other spoke city. Thirty spokes connect 465.

In Tailwinds that connecting traffic often decides whether a spoke is marginal or solidly profitable. A thin route that could never survive on local demand becomes viable when it also feeds your long-haul departures.

How real airlines use hubs

Until the United States deregulated its airlines in 1978, a regulator decided which routes each airline could fly, and most networks ran point to point. Once airlines could choose for themselves, many rebuilt their networks around hubs. Delta, serving smaller cities through Atlanta, was the first to run such a system.

A hub that one airline dominates is called a fortress hub. Delta carried roughly three-quarters of the passengers departing Atlanta in 2024, and American roughly two-thirds of those departing Dallas/Fort Worth. With that share an airline can charge more, because local passengers have few alternatives. That is the reason for the advice below: build your fortress where rivals are thin, and leave theirs alone.

Real hubs schedule flights in banks, with many arrivals close together followed by many departures, so that connections are short. Doha, the hub of Qatar Airways, runs three such waves a day and connected about 30 million passengers in 2019. Tailwinds leaves the clock out. Connections are worked out from weekly frequency, so what matters is how many flights you offer, not when they leave.

Choosing your first hub

Airports in Tailwinds come in three tiers — mega, major, and regional. Major and regional are set from each airport's real passenger traffic; mega is reserved for the world's biggest hubs. (In a Randomized-demand game an airport's roll can move it up or down a tier.) The tier sets gate rent, landing fees and fuel prices, and the mega and major airports are where rival airlines build their networks. It does not set demand — that comes from the city. Bigger sounds better, but the best first hub is usually not the biggest one available. Wherever you found your airline becomes your first Hub, and its country becomes your home country. Look for four things:

  • Strong local demand. Metro population drives the demand model. You want a real city behind your hub so your routes earn even before connections kick in. Some airports also punch above their population because they're tourism magnets or a country's primary international gateway — the game models both. A city with several airports is one market: London's airports share London's passengers, with the secondary fields drawing a smaller slice.
  • Geography that supports spokes. Count the cities within roughly 500–1,500 km. That ring is where your first narrow-body and turboprop routes will live. A hub in the middle of a dense region beats a bigger airport on the edge of nowhere.
  • Room to grow. At mega-hubs you'll fight established AI carriers from day one. A busy major-tier airport with lighter competition frequently earns more, sooner, with less drama.
  • A believable long-haul future. Eventually your hub will host intercontinental routes. A hub that can one day fill a widebody to another continent is worth a small compromise today.
Tip: leave the mega-hubs for later. Beating an established competitor at its own fortress hub takes cash you don't have yet, so build yours where rivals are thin.

Hub tiers: what you build at the hub

Your own connecting itineraries are only sold through airports designated on the Hubs screen, and the designation comes in four steps. Each needs more gates and routes, costs more to build and to run every week, and pays you back with more attractive connections, more connecting seats and a bigger quality bonus on its routes:

  • Focus City — 5 gates and $1M. The only tier you can open outside your home country, and only one per foreign country.
  • Hub — 10 gates, 4 routes at the airport and $5M. Full hubs must be in your home country.
  • Major Hub — 15 gates, 20 routes, 2 international destinations and $25M. From here your own line maintenance discounts the aircraft that fly through.
  • International Gateway — 20 gates, 50 routes, 6 international destinations, $100M, half a year as a Major Hub and 1,000 connecting passengers a week.

Upgrades take time to build — four weeks for a Hub, eight for a Major Hub, sixteen for an International Gateway (a Focus City is instant) — and each tier carries a weekly running cost on top of gate fees, so start one a little before you need it.

Designated stations also make your flying cheaper there: ground handling and catering cost less, and crew layovers cost much less, with the discounts growing at each tier. Gates set hard limits too: each gate you lease covers 50 weekly departures at that airport, and a route can't add frequency beyond what your gates at both ends allow. At a designated hub they matter a second way. Each tier handles a set number of weekly departures per gate, always fewer than 50. Run more flights than your gates can handle and the hub loses some of its connecting traffic until you buy more gates or trim frequency. Your local passengers are not affected.

Growing the hub: rings and spokes

Once the hub is chosen, growth follows a rhythm:

  1. Ring one — short spokes. Open 3–6 routes to nearby cities with regional jets or turboprops. These prove demand, generate steady cash, and start building the feed pool. The turboprop guide covers the cheapest aircraft to do this with.
  2. Ring two — medium haul. Add narrow-body routes to bigger cities 1,500–4,000 km out. Each new spoke makes every existing spoke slightly more valuable through connections.
  3. Trunk routes. When feed is deep enough, open long-haul with a widebody. This is where hub economics pay off: the widebody fills with a blend of local demand and connecting passengers your spokes have been accumulating.

Watch each spoke's load factor as the network grows. A spoke that was marginal at five network destinations often climbs steadily as you add more, because it's carrying an increasing share of connecting traffic. Give new spokes time to mature before judging them — a new route takes about 16 weeks to reach its full demand.

Defending the hub

Success attracts attention. Tailwinds' AI competitors notice profitable markets and move in — the game models route encroachment directly. Your hub is the one place you should never cede ground:

  • Frequency beats size defensively. Three daily narrow-body flights are harder for a competitor to displace than one widebody flight, because passengers weigh the number of departures, not just the seat count.
  • Reputation and loyalty compound at hubs. Cabin quality, catering, and a loyalty program all shift share on contested routes. Passengers with your loyalty status pick you at equal fares — most of your repeat customers live at your hub.
  • Don't hand them an invitation. Rivals look hardest at routes you fly nearly full at fares well above the market reference. An entrant that gets in tends to stay. It only backs off after about five months of fares below the reference, and even then it keeps around half your frequency at a small undercut. Push fares well above the reference again and it comes back fighting. At the hub, fair fares and enough frequency to keep loads below the brim are cheaper than a fight.
  • Don't overreact elsewhere. If a competitor attacks a spoke, decide whether it's strategic (feed for your long-haul) or expendable. You cannot afford to defend every route.

The second hub: when and where

The most common expansion mistake is opening a second hub too early. A second hub splits your fleet, your cash, and your attention. Open one only when all three are true:

  • Your first hub is saturated — the good spokes are flown, frequencies are healthy, and additional aircraft there face diminishing returns.
  • You have a cash cushion that can absorb months of the new hub losing money while its network matures. A new hub loses money at first, so the question is how many months of that you can fund.
  • The new hub complements the old one. The ideal second hub sits far enough away to open a new catchment, close enough to connect with a profitable trunk route between the two. That trunk becomes the spine of your airline — fed at both ends, it's often the most profitable route you'll ever fly.

Geographically, think in pairs a continent apart, or one hub each on two sides of a dense region. Full hubs must be in your home country, so a continent-wide pair is easiest from a big country. Abroad, you are limited to one Focus City per country. Two hubs 700 km apart mostly cannibalise each other's catchments.

Common hub mistakes

  1. Starting at a mega-hub because it's famous. Early on, the number of rivals you face matters more than the size of the airport.
  2. Point-to-point sprawl. Routes scattered across the map with no common endpoint earn only local demand and are individually fragile. Concentrate.
  3. Long-haul before feed. A widebody launched from a hub with three spokes flies mostly empty. Build the feeder routes first.
  4. Ignoring the trunk between hubs. If you run two hubs and don't connect them generously, you're running two small airlines instead of one big one.
