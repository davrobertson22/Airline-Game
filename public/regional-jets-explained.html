Regional Jets Explained

A regional jet is a small jet airliner, usually with fewer than 100 seats, built for routes too thin to fill a 737 or an A320. This article covers what counts as one, the main families, the pilot-contract rule that fixes their size in the United States, and why the smallest ones are disappearing.

By the Tailwinds team · Last updated November 9, 2026

What counts as a regional jet

There is no official definition. "Regional jet" is industry shorthand, and the line moves depending on who draws it. Most sources say fewer than 100 seats. Boeing has drawn it at 90. The trade publication FlightGlobal counts Embraer's E-Jets as regional although the largest seats 146, and treats the similar-sized Airbus A220 as a mainline aircraft.

In practice the name describes a job more than a size. A regional jet flies short routes with modest demand, very often for a regional airline that feeds passengers into a larger carrier's hub under that carrier's brand.

A short history

Small jets are as old as jet travel. The French Caravelle of 1959, with 80 to 140 seats, is usually counted as the first. The BAC One-Eleven, the Soviet Yak-40 and the Fokker F28 followed in the 1960s, the BAe 146 in 1983 and the Fokker 100 in 1988.

The modern regional jet dates from October 1992, when Lufthansa CityLine took delivery of the first Canadair Regional Jet, a 50-seater. Embraer's rival, the ERJ 145, followed in December 1996. Embraer then moved up a size with the E-Jet family in 2004, which seats 66 to 124. More than 1,700 have been built.

Why the 50-seat jet is disappearing

A 50-seat jet needs two pilots and two engines to carry fifty fares. When fuel prices rose in the 2000s, airlines moved to larger regional jets that spread those costs over more seats. Demand for 50-seaters fell, and so did their resale value. Bombardier delivered its last 50-seat CRJ in 2006. By August 2019 about 1,100 of the 50-seat jets were still flying worldwide, 700 of them in the United States, and many were expected to be scrapped.

The fuel arithmetic shows in the table further down. Among the Western jets of the last thirty years, fuel per seat falls as the cabin grows, from about 5¢ a kilometre in a 50-seat ERJ-145 to under 3¢ in a 132-seat E195-E2.

The scope clause: why US regional jets stop at 76 seats

In the United States the size of a regional jet is set by labour contracts as much as by engineering. A scope clause is a term in the contract between a major airline and its pilots' union that limits how many aircraft, and how large, the airline may hand to a regional partner. The aim is to protect better-paid jobs at the main airline. Since 2012 the limit at American, Delta and United has been 76 seats and a maximum take-off weight of 86,000 lb (39 tonnes).

The rule shapes what gets built and bought. US airlines ordered nearly 400 Embraer E175s between 2013 and 2017, because the aircraft fits the limit. Its replacement, the E175-E2, is about 5,400 kg over the weight limit, and Embraer has postponed it past 2027 for lack of orders. In 2019 Bombardier launched the CRJ550, a CRJ700 with only 50 seats and a lower certified weight, built to fit under an airline's limits on larger jets.

Who builds regional jets now

The field has narrowed. Bombardier sold the CRJ programme to Mitsubishi Heavy Industries in a deal that closed on 1 June 2020, and production of the CRJ700, 900 and 1000 ended that year. Mitsubishi's own design, the SpaceJet, first flew in November 2015, was suspended in October 2020 and was cancelled in February 2023.

That leaves Embraer as the only large independent maker. Its re-engined E2 family entered service with the Norwegian airline Widerøe on 24 April 2018, and the E190-E2 burns 17.3% less fuel than the E190 it replaces. China's Comac builds the C909, formerly called the ARJ21. Production of Russia's Superjet 100 has been suspended since 2022. In the United States, the E175 is the only jet still in production that fits the scope clauses.

Regional jet or turboprop?

On the shortest routes a turboprop does the same job for less. A propeller is more efficient than a jet at low speed, so on a sector of a few hundred kilometres an ATR 72 burns roughly a quarter less fuel than a regional jet of similar size in the game's data, and the jet's extra speed saves only minutes. Jets earn their place as the sector gets longer. Our turboprop guide has the figures for every type.
